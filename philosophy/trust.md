# Trust

I was taught that, when you want to build something but do not know where to begin, one of the first questions you should ask is what you absolutely do not want that system to become.

When I think about trust, the first image that comes to mind is the [Trojan Horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_horse).

The story forces us to confront one of the hardest objections against trust: human intelligence is capable of turning trust itself into a weapon. Troy did not fall because its walls were weak. It fell because something hostile was allowed to cross them disguised as a gift.

We often speak of mistrust as though it were inherently harmful. Yet the Trojan Horse reminds us that blind trust can be equally destructive. A system that trusts everything cannot protect itself, because anything capable of presenting itself as harmless may be allowed inside.

But this is not an argument against trust.

It is the strongest argument that trust must overcome.

The lesson should not be that cooperation is impossible, nor that every participant must be treated as an enemy. A world built entirely upon suspicion eventually loses the ability to coordinate, share knowledge, or create anything collectively.

The real challenge is therefore not to eliminate trust, but to build systems in which trust does not require blindness.

Trust should be earned, limited, observable, and revocable. A system should be capable of accepting help without surrendering its integrity; of cooperating with others without assuming that every contribution is harmless; and of detecting betrayal without treating every mistake as an act of hostility.

The Trojan Horse demonstrates that trust can be exploited.

What we build must demonstrate that trust can survive knowing this.

## The importance of leaving traces

We often prefer building code over documenting it. This can happen for many reasons.

Documentation takes time. It rarely feels as rewarding as making something work, and its value may not become visible until the person who wrote the code is no longer available to explain it. When development moves quickly, documenting decisions can feel like an interruption rather than part of the work itself.

**But undocumented systems depend on memory.**

They depend on someone remembering why a decision was made, what alternatives were rejected, which assumptions were considered safe, and which limitations were knowingly accepted. As people leave, projects evolve, and circumstances change, that knowledge gradually disappears.

The code may remain, but the reasoning behind it does not.

This creates a problem for trust. It is difficult to trust a system when we cannot reconstruct how it came to exist. We may see what the system does today, but not why it behaves that way, who introduced a particular decision, or whether an unusual behavior is intentional, accidental, or malicious.

**Documentation leaves traces.**

A trace does not need to contain every thought or every intermediate action. Its purpose is to preserve enough context for another person to understand what happened, question it, and continue from it without depending entirely on the original author.

This includes not only descriptions of what the system does, but also records of decisions, assumptions, changes, failures, rejected approaches, and unresolved concerns.

Leaving traces transforms trust from an act of faith into something that can be examined.

Instead of asking others to believe that a decision was reasonable, we allow them to inspect the reasoning behind it. Instead of expecting future contributors to guess our intentions, we leave evidence they can challenge, correct, or improve.
